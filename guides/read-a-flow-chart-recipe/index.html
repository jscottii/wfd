Flow chart recipes: see a whole recipe at a glance

Why a recipe drawn as a chart is easier to cook from, and how to read one.

A normal recipe is a list of ingredients followed by a block of steps. While cooking, you keep jumping back and forth: how much flour again? Which bowl does the butter go in? A flow chart recipe (sometimes called a tabular recipe) puts both in one picture.

How to read it

Ingredients run down the left side. Each step is a box to the right of the ingredients it uses. When a step combines things, its box stretches across all of their rows, so you can see at a glance what gets mixed with what. Read from left to right: each column happens after the one before it.

Why it helps

  • You can see the parallel work. Two separate branches (say, a sauce and a filling) can be made at the same time, in either order.
  • Amounts are next to the step that uses them, so there's less scanning back up the page.
  • Setup comes first. Steps like "preheat the oven" or "grease the pan" sit above the chart so they aren't forgotten.

When a list is better

Long recipes with many separate stages can get too wide for a phone screen. For those, a top-to-bottom version works better: each step in order, with small tags showing which ingredients join in at that step.
